\chapter{Experimental Evaluation and Results\label{cha:chapter8}}

The models and theories in Chapter~5 and~6 each predict a different class of the system's behavior, which can be summarized as: a congestion-limited scalable throughput according to Chapter~5's steady-state model and a dual-channel fault recovery according to Chapter~6's fault taxonomy. Each prediction is evaluated within this chapter using eighteen independent simulation runs collected from the WSL2/Gazebo Harmonic and Nav2 stack described in Chapter~7. Section~8.2 evaluates the throughput scalability predicted in Chapter~5, while Sections~8.3--8.5 focus on the fault tolerance, coverage, and perception-latency aspects of Chapter~6's recovery model. Finally, a single anomalous run is described in Section~8.6 demonstrating the combined failure mode of both the steady-state and the fault-tolerant models described earlier. Section~8.7 discusses the experimental results in detail, isolating the cases where the actual throughput deviates from the predictions of the steady-state/fault-tolerant models.

\section{Experimental Protocol and Controlled Isolation of Baseline Configurations}

A naive approach to evaluating the fleet's behavior at $N=1$, $N=2$, and $N=3$ robots would be to launch three independent instances of \texttt{multi\_robot\_controlled\_launch.py} containing only the robots under evaluation. However, such an approach would introduce a conflation between the fleet size and the characteristics of the DDS discovery graph/physics engine/CPU utilization on the WSL2 host running the simulation: Each launched Gazebo model increases the load on the host computer regardless of whether the robot is allowed to accept navigation goals~\cite{kronauer2021latency}. The WSL2's limited CPU resources are already a limiting factor for the bring-up reliability as described by Section~7.2.3~\cite{paul2024performance} -- varying the number of spawned robots between the configurations would make it impossible to reason about the differences in throughput due to coordination policy changes rather than computational contention.

Instead, the fleet size is varied by launching all three robots in every run and utilizing the same fail-stop declaration channel described in Section~6.1.2. The run terminates robots not under evaluation at approximately $t \approx 0\,\text{s}$ using the operator dashboard's failure-injection panel (Section~7.6.1). The panel publishes a \texttt{FAILED} status message to the target robot's \texttt{/\{robot\}/status} topic before any \texttt{NavigateToPose} goal is emitted by the task manager, which guarantees that the preempted robot will not leave its spawn coordinates. This creates a baseline configuration where all fleet cardinality-related differences in the discovery graph, SDF world, or CPU utilization are eliminated apart from the number of robots actively accepting navigation goals, which is formally the only variable affecting the outcomes of the compared $N=1$, $N=2$, and $N=3$ configurations~\cite{ribeiro2025indoor,francis2023benchmarking}. A robot process terminated at its spawn coordinate does not project a circular occlusion region as defined by Eq.~\eqref{eq:occlusion_bubble_definition} since none of the three spawn coordinates, $(0,1)$, $(0,0)$, and $(0,-1)$, are placed within an inspection corridor. Therefore, the path-planning logic described in Section~6.4 is not utilized in the baseline configurations of Section~8.2 and only takes effect when a preempted robot is injected mid-mission in Sections~8.3--8.6.

Another consequence of terminating a robot at its spawn coordinate is the change in the node topology of the running processes. \texttt{\_execute\_failure\_cleanup} inside \texttt{waypoint\_sender.py} cancels the robot's mobility goal, deallocates spatial claims, and terminates the motion as if it failed due to internal reasons, but it does not shut down the underlying \texttt{amcl}, \texttt{planner\_server}, \texttt{controller\_server}, \texttt{bt\_navigator}, and \texttt{ekf\_filter\_node} nodes launched by \texttt{multi\_robot\_controlled\_launch.py}. All of these processes remain suspended ROS~2 nodes throughout the execution of the simulation. Consequently, the set of processes running inside the WSL2 container is identical for the evaluated $N=1$, $N=2$, and $N=3$ configurations. Another potential source of variation between the configurations is the computational footprint of the idle processes, but it was not measured separately in this set of experiments.

Finally, a third change introduced by utilizing a single baseline configuration is the adjustment to the battery consumption model described by Eq.~\eqref{eq:hybrid_decay_discrete}. Using the $N=1$ mission duration ($\bar{t}_{\text{TAT}} = 2443.72\,\text{s}$) and distance ($\bar{d} = 453.82\,\text{m}$) from Section~8.2 along with the coefficients $\lambda_{\text{idle}} = 0.02\,\%\,\text{s}^{-1}$ and $\eta_{\text{dynamic}} = 0.15\,\%\,\text{m}^{-1}$ yields the expected battery level at the end of the mission as
\begin{equation}
\lambda_{\text{idle}} \bar{t}_{\text{TAT}} + \eta_{\text{dynamic}} \bar{d} = 48.87\,\% + 68.07\,\% \approx 116.9\,\% \label{eq:n1_discharge_overshoot}
\end{equation}
exceeding the $100\,\%$ charge budget of Eq.~\eqref{eq:absolute_failure_threshold} roughly $85.5\,\%$ of the way through the mission, near the $121^{\text{st}}$ of $142$ waypoints. Chapter~6's fail-stop policy does not distinguish between a robot whose batteries ran out due to insufficient capacity in the single-robot baseline configuration and a robot whose motion controllers failed midway through the multi-robot mission. Failure to differentiate between the two is undesirable since an appropriately sized single-robot baseline should never reach the absolute failure threshold from Eq.~\eqref{eq:absolute_failure_threshold}. Therefore, the discharging coefficients are set to zero for the nominal runs evaluated in Section~8.2, and the coefficients $\lambda_{\text{idle}}$, $\eta_{\text{dynamic}}$ are reverted to the values used for the fault-tolerant runs only in Sections~8.3--8.6 where the energy exhaustion is not an expected outcome. The complete eighteen-run experimental campaign structure—partitioned into nominal fleet scaling baselines $(N=1,2,3)$ and timed mid-mission failure injection benchmarks $(25\%, 50\%, 75\%)$—is systematically organized as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:ch8_experimental_protocol}.

\section{Nominal Scalability Analysis: Throughput versus Fleet Size}

Within Chapter~5, the coordination layer for the fleet was proposed, which takes the form of a decentralized, spatially partitioned, mutual exclusion protocol~\cite{pereira2023multi}. Accordingly, these nominal runs were performed to assess it against the ideal $N$-times throughput, which would be achieved with parallel, conflict-free division of labor, recorded using Table~\ref{tab:nominal_tat_scaling} below. It summarizes for each configuration ($N=1$, $N=2$, $N=3$), the achieved audit time (TAT) for each of the three runs, with respect to the isolation protocol, as specified in Section~8.1, plus the speedup $S_N = \bar{t}_{\text{TAT}}(1)/\bar{t}_{\text{TAT}}(N)$, parallel efficiency
\begin{equation}
\eta_N = \frac{S_N}{N} = \frac{\bar{t}_{\text{TAT}}(1)}{N \cdot \bar{t}_{\text{TAT}}(N)} \label{eq:parallel_efficiency}
\end{equation}
as derived from standard parallel workload division theory~\cite{ribeiro2025parallel}.

Thus, the $99.4\,\%$ efficiency at two robots is close to linear scaling, which is justified. Given a claim-renewal period of $T_{\text{renew}} = 1.5\,\text{s}$ and a mission duration on the order of twenty minutes, the mutual exclusion protocol overheads should theoretically be negligible for such a small fleet. This is indeed the case, and it can be explained by the fact that, according to \texttt{owned\_regions} in \texttt{config.py}, \texttt{robot1} and \texttt{robot2} only share the fourth Cartesian region, and therefore are mostly independent in terms of the twelve common corridors specified in Section~5.2.3.

Meanwhile, already with three robots, the efficiency drops to $90.3\,\%$, which is a $9\,\%$ decrease compared to $N=2$. This drop cannot be explained by the simple addition of the third robot's renewal and claim costs alone. Rather, it stems from the topological arrangement of the assigned regions: \texttt{robot1}'s $\{2, 1, 4\}$ overlaps with \texttt{robot2}'s $\{4, 3\}$ on region~4, and with \texttt{robot3}'s $\{2, 3\}$ on region~2, while \texttt{robot2} and \texttt{robot3} also overlap on region~3. Consequently, at $N=3$, all three pairwise overlaps become active simultaneously, tripling the number of contested region boundaries instead of merely adding one. While this run-to-run variation does not follow a monotonic trend across growing fleet sizes, the coefficient of variation is recorded at $0.80\,\%$ for $N=1$, $0.54\,\%$ for $N=2$, and $2.56\,\%$ for $N=3$. Given this run-to-run variation estimate is based on only three runs per fleet size, the small difference between the mean across $N=1$ and $N=2$ should not be overinterpreted, while the significant difference at $N=3$ may be considered indicative of a true variation increase on the part of the three-robot fleet. This is consistent with the hypothesis of the non-deterministic grant sequencing introduced by the asymmetric precedence relation of Eq.~\eqref{eq:asymmetric_precedence} leading to an increased variance in the system compared to two robots in a mutual exclusion scenario, as three robots now have an opportunity to interfere with one another in grant sequencing compared to only two at a time.

\section{Failure-to-Reallocation Latency (FTL) Characterization}

Section~6.1.2 contains two channels through which robots report their failures to the fleet's reallocation logic: an explicit self-declaration channel, whereby a robot publishes a \texttt{FAILED} payload as soon as it detects a hardware failure, and a passive heartbeat-timeout channel, whereby a robot's absence from the heartbeat topic for $T_{\text{timeout}} = 5.0\,\text{s}$ triggers Eq.~\eqref{eq:heartbeat_timeout_formula}~\cite{matos2025efficient}. In either case, all trials of the present study -- both the startup-phase preemption of Sect.~8.1 and the mid-mission failure injections of Sects.~8.4 and~8.6 -- were initiated from the operator dashboard via the \texttt{inject\_failure} control, thereby publishing a \texttt{FAILED} status directly onto the \texttt{/\{robot\}/status} topic of the target robot. With this caveat, all fifteen FTL entries of Table~\ref{tab:ftl_by_condition} were recorded on the explicit declaration channel; the timeout channel is implemented but was never exercised by this campaign, since it would take effect only for silent process or network crashes rather than the operator-triggered failures tested here. This scope boundary is worth stating plainly rather than implying broader coverage than the fifteen self-declaration samples actually provide.

Two observations concern the pooled distribution. First, note that thirteen out of fifteen entries are within $0.9\,\text{ms}$--$5.8\,\text{ms}$, or three orders of magnitude below $T_{\text{timeout}}$. After removing the two obvious outliers as a heavy tail, the reduced sample has a mean of $2.24\,\text{ms}$ and a maximum of $5.8\,\text{ms}$. The low millisecond range is consistent with the expected detection cost, which dominates the FTL regime in this setup. In other words, the loopback DDS publication, subscription callback, and \texttt{trigger\_reallocation} computation all contribute to the latency budget, without any polling period comparable to $T_{\text{renew}} = 1.5\,\text{s}$ or $T_{\text{timeout}} = 5.0\,\text{s}$. The explicit declaration channel does not appear rate-constrained, and the sample supports the qualitative claim of Sect.~6.1.2 that decentralized detection latency is ``low under reliable connectivity,'' at least for the channel under investigation.

Second, note that the $N=1$ and $75\,\%$ injection condition trials of Sect.~8.6, at $477.3\,\text{ms}$ and $215.5\,\text{ms}$ respectively, exhibit an approximately twofold increase in FTL compared to the bulk of the sample. Being an order of magnitude below $T_{\text{timeout}}$, these values do not affect the safety argument of Sect.~6.1.3 that reallocation finishes long before the five-second deadline. Nevertheless, they illustrate a heavier tail for the FTL distribution than suggested by the median-only value of $1.8\,\text{ms}$. Section~7.2.1 highlights that WSL2's virtualization software adds significant variance to the underlying Linux kernel when scheduling Gazebo, Nav2, and the ROS~2 executor concurrently. It is plausible, though not certain, that the same source explains the two outliers, as the sporadic delay in the executor thread would prolong the subscription callback's processing time, thus delaying \texttt{trigger\_reallocation}. The evidence on which this statement is based is entirely consistent with an alternative explanation that one of the two failed robots was slower at advertising its \texttt{FAILED} status, but no such hypothesis can be formulated with greater confidence than any other.

A final note pertains to the coordinator field recorded alongside the FTL values in Table~\ref{tab:ftl_by_condition}. Out of the nine mid-mission injection trials of Table~\ref{tab:ftl_by_condition}, the surviving pair of robots alternated in leadership, or the role of processing the failed peer's status message and invoking \texttt{trigger\_reallocation}. Specifically, \texttt{robot3} declared itself the coordinator six times and \texttt{robot1} three times, with the same robot winning in consecutive trials within a given injection-timing block on several occasions, most notably all three $75\,\%$ trials. This behavior is expected from the masterless design of the recovery controller described in Sect.~6.1.4: which surviving robot subscribes to the failed peer's \texttt{/\{robot\}/status} topic first determines the outcome of the race, and the idempotency guarantee of Eq.~\eqref{eq:idempotency_proof} ensures that the first call to \texttt{trigger\_reallocation} wins in all subsequent ties. The winner's processing of the failed peer's status message is not instantaneous, however; in the $N=1$ condition of Table~\ref{tab:ftl_by_condition}, two robots are scheduled to fail before the mission starts, but only one latency entry is recorded per run. This consistency is due to \texttt{MultiRobotSystemDiagnosis.\_write\_reallocation\_latency} in \texttt{diagnosis.py} being invoked only once per detected failure, as the second failure occurs nearly simultaneously with the \texttt{waypoint\_sender} node restarting the first failed robot. In reality, \texttt{waypoint\_sender}'s reallocation logic splits tasks across both of the surviving robots correctly, but the diagnostics script's \texttt{\_write\_reallocation\_latency} guard misses the second, near-simultaneous \texttt{trigger\_reallocation} call entirely. Consequently, the three $N=1$ entries of Table~\ref{tab:ftl_by_condition} should be interpreted as the time between the status advertising of the failed robots and the first successful \texttt{trigger\_reallocation}, not the second, if it occurred.

As expected, the mission duration decreases with each successive injection point, being $1216.09\,\text{s}$, $1187.87\,\text{s}$, and $1052.96\,\text{s}$ for the $25\,\%$, $50\,\%$, and $75\,\%$ conditions respectively, with no repetition of any condition producing a shorter mission than any repetition of an earlier condition. The cause of this trend is explained by the resizing of the unallocated target pool $\mathcal{U}$ passed to \texttt{trigger\_reallocation} as the progress of the mission increases with the injection point: at $25\,\%$, there are approximately 106 targets remaining in $\mathcal{U}$ on average across repetitions, compared to 35 at $75\,\%$, leaving less work for the two survivor robots to split between themselves according to the \texttt{battery\_proportional\_ratio} of Eq.~\eqref{eq:battery_proportional_ratio}~\cite{hamidoglu2026survey}. However, in none of the three fault-injected conditions does the duration match the $901.71\,\text{s}$ baseline of an uninterrupted $N=3$ of Section~8.2, with the latest possible injection point still having a mission $16.7\,\%$ slower than the three-robot baseline, as predicted by the qualitative argument at the end of Section~8.4.

Meanwhile, the coverage does not follow a monotonic trend, with the $25\,\%$ and $75\,\%$ conditions both having a mean loss of approximately $0.33$ and $1.0$ waypoints respectively compared to a perfect $142/142$, while the $50\,\%$ condition had a much higher mean loss of $2.33$, and a significantly higher variance: one of its three repetitions, namely Run~\#14, had a perfect $142/142$, while the other two, Run~\#13 and Run~\#15, had a loss of 3 and 4 waypoints respectively, the two largest losses seen in any of the 18 runs of this campaign. Given that \texttt{select\_next\_target} in \texttt{item\_selection.py} has no stochastic component and depends only on the claim state and robot positions, two repetitions having the same state at a certain waypoint count will have a similar (but not necessarily identical) subset of completed tasks at that count. Thus, each repetition of a $50\,\%$ failure injection at the 71st completed waypoint (Run~\#13, Run~\#14, Run~\#15) will have \texttt{robot2} in roughly the same area, with some variation due to differences in the timing of claims between the repetitions: Section~8.6 focuses on Run~\#13 as a representative example of the cluster, analyzing the cause of the coverage loss in the vicinity of a certain corridor; Run~\#15's results are consistent with the same cause but were not instrumented to the same extent and are only mentioned in passing as an additional observation supporting the conclusion.

The proximity-violation counter of Section~7.4.2 flagged exactly one event across all eighteen runs, occurring in the first $25\,\%$ injection repetition, Run~\#10. Given that the proximity monitoring code in \texttt{diagnosis.py} only compares the two most recently received AMCL poses at $1\,\text{Hz}$ and does not retain a continuous minimum-distance trace, the single proximity violation reported across all 18 runs should be read as a coarse lower bound on near-miss frequency rather than a precise characterization of inter-robot clearance. Thus, the observation does not contradict the claim of Section~8.3 that collisions between robots are rare under these conditions.

\section{Perception Pipeline Latency and Analytical Redundancy Validation}

The fault tolerance test of Sections~8.3 and~8.4 only touches on an aspect of the fleet abstraction's dual-channel design, using the fault-injection framework to cause task allocation failures detectable by the channel-monitoring mechanism of Section~6.6; this section similarly demonstrates the second type of failure surface considered in Chapter~6, the classifier failover mechanism of Section~6.7, as well as the relevance of the worst-case-end-to-end latency bound of Eq.~\eqref{eq:wcet_climb_bounds}. The 7278 scan events logged for \texttt{anomaly\_latency.csv} across all 18 runs provide a substantial sample of measurements to build a distribution of the perception pipeline's wall-clock latency~\cite{teper2024thread}.

First, as WCET estimates are typically concerned with the upper bounds of execution time, comparing the distribution to the mechanical scanning window $T_{\text{scan}} \in \{1.5\,\text{s}, 4.0\,\text{s}\}$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:wcet_climb_bounds} serves as a good sanity check to establish that the pipeline latency rarely, if ever, becomes the limiting factor for the fleet coordination loop. Specifically, not a single one of the 7278 samples came close to either of the two thresholds, and the 99th percentile of $32.26\,\text{ms}$ was still nearly fifty times smaller than the smallest threshold. The claim of Section~8.3 that perception latency rarely becomes the binding constraint is thus considerably strengthened by the large sample size of this campaign, as compared to the point estimates of Chapter~5. Meanwhile, the per-robot breakdown only shows a small variance between the three agents: the means of $9.93\,\text{ms}$, $10.72\,\text{ms}$, and $10.14\,\text{ms}$ fell within the same order of magnitude for all three, as expected from the homogeneous-fleet assumption of Section~4.2.

However, an apparent discrepancy arises when comparing the latency measurements to the complexity estimates of Section~5.5.4: specifically, the approximately $\approx 1.2\,\text{ms}$ execution time advertised for the entire perception pipeline conflicts with the $10.17\,\text{ms}$ mean measured between the \texttt{\_run\_async\_scanning\-\_pipeline} timestamps. A closer examination of the code reveals that the discrepancy is caused by the pipeline's stages differing between the two measurements: the complexity analysis considers only the vision-processing stages and not the host computer's I/O, whereas the \texttt{\_run\_async\_scanning\_pipeline} Python coroutine actually measures the time from before \texttt{cv2.imread} to after the classifier outputs a probability, thus including the image loading from disk, the resizing operation, and the three-stage spatial augmentation pipeline (illumination scaling, affine rotation, and additive Gaussian noise).

Therefore, the approximately eight-times difference between the two numbers should not be interpreted as an error in either analysis, but rather as a consequence of measuring different aspects of the pipeline. Eq.~\eqref{eq:total_pipeline_complexity} only bounds the complexity of the feature transformation and classification stages, while Table~\ref{tab:perception_latency} lists the latencies of the entire end-to-end scan processing pipeline, including the aforementioned extra stages. Both measurements are correct within their context but should not be conflated into a single ``pipeline latency'' figure when citing or comparing the results. A similar caveat applies to the discrepancy between the $1.2\,\text{ms}$ complexity estimate and the $10\,\text{ms}$ mean in Table~\ref{tab:perception_latency}: the complexity only accounts for the vision modules and not the host computer's processing of the raw sensor data in general.

Another observation stemming from the distribution of the 7278 measurements is the clear skewness of the distribution: with the mean of $10.17\,\text{ms}$ being approximately thirteen percent higher than the median of $9.01\,\text{ms}$, and the maximum measurement of $132.06\,\text{ms}$ being nearly thirteen times higher than the mean. One explanation, similar to the one offered in Section~8.3 for the distribution of the task allocation latencies, is the host operating system's inherent variability in process scheduling, which ultimately impacts the timestamp measurements recorded in \texttt{anomaly\_latency.csv}. However, the WSL2 host of these experiments is also responsible for filesystem caching, which could have confounded the latency measurements in the \texttt{run\_async\_scanning\_pipeline} coroutine: the disk reads for the ground-truth dataset images may have been served from memory in some repetitions and from the physical storage in others, artificially varying the measurement.

Finally, every one of the 7278 scan events processed in this campaign used the SVM classifier, with none of the repetitions triggering the \texttt{XGBoost} fallback described in Section~6.7.1. This outcome is consistent with the primary classifier not raising any exceptions throughout eighteen runs, but it also means that the analytical redundancy mechanism advertised in Section~6.7 was never tested by this campaign: the $t_{\text{fallback}} \le 5.0\,\text{ms}$ bound of Eq.~\eqref{eq:fallback_processing_time} only concerns the time needed to process a scan event with the fallback classifier, but no scan event in these runs had to be reprocessed due to an exception thrown by the primary classifier. Similarly, the shared preprocessing SPOF warned about in Section~6.7.2 is a hypothetical issue in this context, as none of the runs in this dataset encountered the numerical instability scenario described there.

The analytical redundancy mechanism and the preprocessing SPOF both require further testing, ideally through a dedicated fault-injection campaign targeting the perception pipeline's internal Python objects and deliberately corrupting the classifier inputs or weights in order to trigger an exception. This direction for future research is identified in Chapter~9, but it is not pursued here due to the time constraints of this thesis.

\section{Case Study: Corridor Occlusion and Blacklist-Induced Coverage Deficit (Run \#13)}

The only timing condition in Table~\ref{tab:injection_timing_summary} with an inter-run coverage loss of more than $50\,\%$ and two individual runs (Run~\#13, Run~\#15) also losing more than one waypoint is the $50\,\%$ timing condition -- and the one under investigation (Run~\#13) is the more fully instrumented one of the two. When \texttt{robot2} was manually failed, the entire fleet had completed $72/142$ targets; with \texttt{robot1} and \texttt{robot3} alone, the mission was completed at $1168.45\,\text{s}$, $139/142$ ($97.89\,\%$) of waypoints inspected, with \texttt{robot3} as the coordinator and an FTL of $1.0\,\text{ms}$. This TAT is notably better than the $50\,\%$ condition's average of $1187.87\,\text{s}$, despite a substantially worse coverage deficit, due to an interaction between the size of the surviving robot's task queue at the moment a robot is failed over the horizon and the \texttt{RobotState.IDLE} transition. The smaller the distance to be traversed at the end of the mission, the shorter the TAT, irrespective of its cause.

The operator's live diagnostic trace for the run in question puts \texttt{robot2}'s final AMCL pose at approximately $(11.61, -15.06)$ in the global coordinate frame at the moment of failure. Given the \texttt{shelf\_9}'s SDF pose $y \approx -16.44$ and \texttt{shelf\_10}'s $y \approx -14.10$ (\texttt{tugbot\_warehouse\_clean.txt}), this localizes \texttt{robot2} to the narrow aisle between them, \texttt{shelf\_9\_yplus} paired with \texttt{shelf\_10\_yminus} under the key \texttt{corridor\_small\_corner\_top} in the \texttt{SHARED\_CORRIDORS} mapping of \texttt{config.py}, one of nine small-shelf narrow aisles named there. This corridor class is the one Section~6.2.1 identifies as constraining the fleet's narrowest physical margin, $W_{\text{aisle}} = 2.25\,\text{m}$~\cite{silva2024agvs}, the same constant used there to bound the acceptable AMCL covariance gate. Thus, a robot failure in this specific corridor represents an operator's demonstration of the system's worst-case behavior within the designed operating envelope.

The impact of this failure on the surviving robots' ability to complete inspections for \texttt{shelf\_9\_yplus} is governed by the Pythagorean parallel-axis shifting dynamics of Section~6.4.2. Since both \texttt{shelf\_9} and \texttt{shelf\_10} are small, with their yplus/yminus approach axis aligned to the global Y-axis (up to a 180-degree rotation), Case~1 of that section applies to both. The blocked approach point is therefore shifted along X by $\Delta x = \sqrt{D_{\text{safe}}^2 - \Delta y^2}$ (Eq.~\eqref{eq:pythagorean_x_shift}) to find a new inspection point, subject to the small shelf's X displacement limit of $|\Delta x| \le 1.8\,\text{m}$ (Eq.~\eqref{eq:small_shelf_shift_bound}) and the perspective 35-degree gate (Eq.~\eqref{eq:perspective_validation_gate}). Thus, the occlusion bubble of radius $D_{\text{safe}} = 0.85\,\text{m}$ (Eq.~\eqref{eq:occlusion_bubble_definition}) centered at \texttt{robot2}'s failed position consumes about $37.8\,\%$ of the $2.25\,\text{m}$-wide aisle by itself, before any allowance for the surviving robot's own footprint. Had the same event happened in one of the wider big-shelf corridors where the approach offset is $2.25\,\text{m}$ rather than the $1.5\,\text{m}$ afforded to small shelves (\texttt{BIG\_SHELF\_SPECS} versus \texttt{SMALL\_SHELF\_SPECS} in \texttt{config.py}), a shifted goal would have proportionally more room to simultaneously clear $D_{\text{safe}}$, respect $X_{\text{limit}}$, and remain within the perspective gate.

However, in an ultra-narrow $2.25\,\text{m}$ aisle, particularly the small shelf's, all three constraints ($\Delta x$, perspective gate, and new robot occlusion bubble) are more likely to collide, which is the root cause of the coverage deficit in Run~\#13. Specifically, whenever \texttt{\_dispatch\_navigation} attempts to shift the inspection point and fails to find a point satisfying all constraints (or fails to navigate to it), \texttt{\_defer\_active\_task} is called, incrementing the per-side deferral counter.

With $\tau_{\text{blacklist}} = 2$, two such blacklist entries would cause permanent task queue pruning across all robots on that shelfside. Since blacklist propagation happens per-side (not per-section), whichever sections on \texttt{shelf\_9\_yplus} were not yet claimed by any robot at the time \texttt{robot2} failed would be permanently dropped (in addition to the ones \texttt{robot2} itself already inspected). However, the \texttt{completed\_waypoints\_global} set is monotonic (Eq.~\eqref{eq:blacklist_monotonicity}), so the already completed sections (if any) by \texttt{robot2} before its failure would not be retracted. Thus, the mission would lose at least one section's worth of waypoints, and potentially more, depending on how many sections \texttt{robot2} had time to inspect before being failed.

This accounts for at least one of the three missing waypoints in Run~\#13's final tally, according to the geometry and the code paths, although possibly not all three. The \texttt{coverage\_loss.csv} and \texttt{total\_audit\_time.csv} files do not show any other per-section completion logging that would have accrued during an additional section occlusion, much less a second one, that would have caused a coverage deficit for this run or any other. To infer such a scenario required attaching per-section corridor identifiers to \texttt{\_defer\_active\_task} and \texttt{publish\_blacklist} at the time of the event, which we leave to Chapter~9. Similarly, while Run~\#15's mission loss of four waypoints under the same $50\,\%$ timing condition may well be explained by an event of this type, it was not analyzed at this level of detail and only appears here as general context for Run~\#13.

\section{Synthesis of Experimental Findings}

Taken together, the results in Sections~8.2--8.6 neither confirm nor completely refute the models in Chapters~5 and~6, as the tests evaluate different aspects of the system's performance. First of all, the nominal scale tests demonstrate that the decentralized spatial mutex is indeed a good coordination primitive for moderate fleet sizes: the $N=2$ configuration achieves $99.4\,\%$ of theoretical peak performance. Meanwhile, the drop in performance on the $N=3$ scenario was not due to a systemic performance issue in the mutex protocol, but rather a particular edge case in region overlaps. Secondly, the fault-tolerance experiments are also largely inconclusive. The explicit self-declaration channel indeed behaves as described in Chapter~6, completing all reallocation tasks within a few milliseconds, thus meeting the five-second SLA target. However, two fault-tolerance mechanisms built into the system did not demonstrate their ability to withstand serious faults. The passive heartbeat-timeout channel (Eq.~\eqref{eq:heartbeat_timeout_formula}) did not experience a single peer failure during eighteen test runs, and thus was unable to demonstrate its ability to recover from one. Likewise, the XGBoost classifier failover mechanism described in Section~6.7.1 did not have a single opportunity to take over from the primary classifier, as none of the $7278$ scans recorded during the test runs triggered a primary-classifier exception. The ability of the system to withstand memory-corruption attacks, feature spoofing, and network degradation under coordinated attack vectors must be further investigated in future hardware-in-the-loop testing and reliability analysis. Finally, it is important to explicitly recognize fault-tolerance mechanisms that have been implemented but have not been demonstrated to be useful, in order to distinguish capabilities (explicitly coded but not exercised) from demonstrated behaviors.

In this light, the findings in Section~8.6 represent the most interesting aspect of the experimental investigation. Rather than discovering a critical design flaw, the only issue found in the test runs was an inherent limitation in the fault-propagation methodology described in Section~6.5.3. Specifically, the coarseness of the blacklist propagation granularity (side-level rather than section-level, Section~6.5.3) turned out to be insufficient to prevent traffic disruptions in the narrowest-corridor formation described in Section~6.2.1. Both the theoretical considerations and the analysis of the data in Section~8.6 suggest that this scenario is not impossible, only improbable, being restricted to some specialized and quite narrow mid-mission transients. Only one of Run~\#13's three lost waypoints could be attributed to this mechanism with confidence; the remaining two are not explained by any record retained from this campaign. This gap is a consequence of the logging granularity available at the time, and it is reported here as an open question rather than a resolved finding. Overall, the experiments in Sections~8.2 through~8.6 confirm Chapter~5's steady-state throughput predictions, as well as Chapter~6's basic principles of fault-tolerant coordination. Specifically, the reactive approach to fault prediction and recovery described in Chapter~6 was demonstrated to be effective and efficient, without impacting the system's nominal performance. However, the experiments also highlight the need for future work on passive fault prediction methods, analytical redundancy, and task tracking at the section level as proposed in Chapter~9.
