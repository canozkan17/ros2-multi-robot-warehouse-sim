\chapter{Conclusions and Future Work\label{cha:chapter9}}

Chapter~4 posed the audit task as a formal coverage problem over a bounded workspace containing 22 storage units and 142 inspection targets. In subsequent chapters, a system was designed, decomposed, implemented, and evaluated to solve it without a human supervisor. This concluding chapter reflects on what has been learned and where this work leaves the reader. It is not enough to say that the solution has been implemented and evaluated -- in Section~\ref{sec:ch9_synthesis} we consider the implications of this work for both the main and the fault-tolerant strands of the thesis, tying them together into a common narrative. We return to the formal statements of Chapter~4 made about the system designed in Chapter~7 in Section~\ref{sec:ch9_revisit}, contrasting them against the empirical findings of Chapter~8 for the claims that have been demonstrated to hold under the scrutiny of eighteen simulation runs. Section~\ref{sec:ch9_engineering} considers the class of failures documented in an engineering log kept throughout development of the system described in Chapter~7, extracting five recurring themes from the negative outcomes as a means to classify the types of failures that may affect similar systems. This section does not merely describe anecdotes; each item has been related to relevant distributed-systems and robotics literature to ground them in the broader context. Finally, Section~\ref{sec:ch9_limits} states the limitations of the current work, while Section~\ref{sec:ch9_future} groups together ideas for future work according to the layered architecture outlined in Section~6.8.1. The organization of Section~\ref{sec:ch9_closing} concludes the thesis.

\section{Synthesis of Contributions Across the Steady-State and Fault-Tolerant Frameworks\label{sec:ch9_synthesis}}

While Chapters 5 and 6 describe two fundamentally different systems, one of which executes steady-state coordination while another recovers from failures, their inner workings are deeply intertwined in ways that go beyond just a few sentences at the boundary. Indeed, elaborating on these connections constitutes the easiest way to synthesize the work described in these chapters.

Specifically, any component in Chapter 6 can be viewed as a monitor that wraps an invariant from Chapter 5. For instance, the hybrid discharge model in Eq.~\eqref{eq:hybrid_decay_discrete} does not participate in the proportional reallocation ratio in Eq.~\eqref{eq:battery_proportional_ratio} as an independent contributor but rather supplies that formula with an estimate of the battery capacity in the nominal case. In other words, the choice of the steady-state energy accounting method dictates the manner in which robots rebalance their loads when one of them fails. The same is true for the spatial mutex in Eq.~\eqref{eq:spatial_availability_check}, which underpins the corridor-clearing motion primitive in Eq.~\eqref{eq:pythagorean_x_shift}. Indeed, Section~5.5 designs the linear SVM in Eq.~\eqref{eq:svm_decision_function} to have a manageable run-time in order to fit within the computational budget of the nominal controller. Thus, the closed-form back-projection in Eq.~\eqref{eq:xai_back_projection} becomes preferable to the alternative one offered by the XGBoost "reserve allocation model" in Section~6.7.1 when numerical stability is of concern. None of the five diagnostic abstractions in Section~6.8.1 function in isolation but rather piggyback on a set of state observers that were engineered in the steady-state coordination layer to avoid redesigning them for the fault-tolerant layer.

This pattern is in fact quite common for decentralized multi-robot systems, where teams of robots that do not possess a global reference frame opportunistically use any additional information that becomes available as a side-effect of executing the nominal coordination logic \cite{zhang2025survey}. In this thesis, such opportunities arose because Chapter 6 did not have the luxury of a centralized recovery manager (cf. Section~5.2.2), and the handful of diagnostics that it employs are repurposed from the same AMCL covariance stream, battery state estimates, and claim arbitration logic that power the nominal controller in Chapter 5. This is what allows Chapter 6 to sidestep the $\mathcal{O}(N^2)$ subscription bottleneck in Eq.~\eqref{eq:network_traffic_scale}, which would have been imposed by a separate diagnostic subscription layer. The computational budget that would have been consumed by running two monitoring stacks (one for coordination, and another for fault tolerance) became unavailable at the CPU level, as described in Section~7.2.3.

The gate-based launch framework in Chapter 7 serves as the enabler for this kind of opportunistic integration, as described in this section. The specific choice of the gate in Section~7.3.2 that waits for the EKF fused odometry topic to appear before launching Nav2 plugins is in large part dictated by the need for Chapter 5's metrics to be repeatable. However, the same design premise allows Chapter 6 to utilize the same AMCL-specific diagnostics to detect covariance blow-ups that are caused by the Nav2 plugins prematurely launching before their transform dependencies are met (cf. Eq.~\eqref{eq:sigma_amcl_derivation}). At the moment when such transients are observed, the launch system has already performed enough filtering to prevent the recovery layer from initiating spurious recovery maneuvers. Thus, the design choices that made Chapter 5's performance characteristics consistent and repeatable are directly responsible for the opportunities that led to the implementation of Chapter 6.

As elaborated in Chapter~8, the benefits of this approach are difficult to overestimate. The almost linear behavior of the curve in $N=2$ ($\eta_2 = 99.4\,\%$, Table~\ref{tab:nominal_tat_scaling}) demonstrates that Chapter 5's coordination logic is light enough not to appreciably affect the recovery layer when it is idle, which it typically is. Meanwhile, the sub-six-millisecond latencies in Table~\ref{tab:ftl_by_condition} demonstrate that when the failure mode enters, the recovery layer will utilize cached state information and avoid incurring additional subscription bandwidth costs. This is precisely the design outcome that was envisioned in Section~6.1.2, and the metrics in Table~\ref{tab:centralized_vs_decentralized_fault} demonstrate that the alternative of centralized arbitration does not offer comparable benefits for a team of three robots. However, the qualitative comparison also illustrates that the diagnostic-driven approach does not incur prohibitive detection latency, has an update period that is appropriate for the idempotent reallocation operator in Eq.~\eqref{eq:idempotency_proof}, and rarely exceeds five seconds of service time (cf. eighteen runs in Table~\ref{tab:centralized_vs_decentralized_fault}), all without violating the $\mathcal{O}(N^2)$ subscription bandwidth budget that is described in Section~6.1.3 as the limiting factor for decentralized multi-robot systems. Thus, Chapters 4 through 8 do not describe two separate contributions in the domain of multi-robot coordination and fault tolerance that happened to use the same shared telemetry foundation. Rather, these chapters describe the opportunity to use one system as the enabler for the other, as well as the manner in which each of these systems was evaluated. Finally, Section~\ref{sec:ch9_revisit} builds on this section to evaluate to what extent the system's characteristics match the design goals that were articulated in Chapter~4.

\section{Revisiting the Problem Formulation: Coverage and Resilience Guarantees Achieved\label{sec:ch9_revisit}}

Prior to any implementation, Chapter~4 established two formal design constraints: a coverage guarantee mandating that the union of all task sets at any given instant form a partition satisfying $\mathcal{P}$ under pairwise-disjoint assignments (Section~4.1.3.2), and a fail-stop depletion model allowing robots to operate until their battery falls to $E_{\text{fail}} = 0.0\,\%$ (Eq.~\eqref{eq:absolute_failure_threshold}) rather than immediately retreating to the dock. Chapter~8 is the only other chapter in this thesis where these guarantees are subjected to empirical evaluation, which motivates identifying which aspects of Chapter~4 were actually tested throughout the eighteen-run campaign.

Under normal circumstances, the problem formulation's coverage guarantee was achieved in its entirety: all three baseline configurations in Section~8.2 report a valid Total Audit Time only after each robot has satisfied \texttt{completed\_waypoints\_count} $\ge$ \texttt{waypoint\_limit} and transitioned to \texttt{RobotState.IDLE} (Section~7.1.2). As such, the $N=1$, $N=2$, and $N=3$ runs in Table~\ref{tab:nominal_tat_scaling} are all complete-coverage executions by design, with the waypoint coverage of the sweep architecture in Section~5.3 never exhibiting an orphaned target in the absence of failures. With respect to the first constraint in Section~4.1.3.2, this outcome certifies the validity of the disjoint-partition formulation; however, the second half of the union condition under failure remains unsubstantiated as a separate claim.

The reason for this discrepancy is that the weaker claim $\mathcal{P}_{\text{active}}(t) \setminus \mathcal{B}_{\text{global}}(t) \neq \emptyset$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:target_reachability_boundary} is strictly about maintaining a non-empty subset of targets after a peer failure occurs, whereas the coverage statement in Section~4.1.3.2 concerns the ability to achieve full coverage even in the face of failures. In fact, Chapter 6 only provides this guarantee in relation to fleet resilience: a three-robot formation can tolerate $|\mathcal{A}| - 1$ failures while continuing to cover the subset of $\mathcal{P}$ that is within reach of the surviving robots (Eq.~\eqref{eq:fleet_survival_boundary}). The coverage statistics in Section~8.4 reveal that the eighteen-run campaign only obeys the weaker guarantee rather than the one explicitly stated in Section~4.1.3.2. Specifically, the mean coverage loss across the three injection-time variants in Table~\ref{tab:injection_timing_summary} only spanned $0.23$--$2.33$ waypoints out of the total 142. In addition, Section~8.6 attributes the single navigation failure in Run~\#13 to the coarse-grained nature of the blacklist injection in Section~6.5.3 rather than the allocation policy's ability to deal with partial-fleet failures. Most importantly, the formal coverage guarantee never stated that no such losses would occur at the time of failures, which means that the loss of up to $2.8\,\%$ of the target set in the worst-case scenario should only be regarded as an empirical observation rather than a provable invariant.

This finding is particularly pertinent to the growing literature on multi-robot coverage that specifically cites distributed, multi-coverage redundancy in the face of local perception limitations as a design constraint \cite{guitouni2026distributed}. In particular, the redundancy guarantees in such works take the form of Eq.~\eqref{eq:fleet_survival_boundary} and Eq.~\eqref{eq:target_reachability_boundary}, with few papers providing a formal proof of the target set coverage in the presence of failures. With this caveat in mind, it becomes evident that neither Eq.~\eqref{eq:fleet_survival_boundary} nor Eq.~\eqref{eq:target_reachability_boundary} are formal guarantees, only design choices that were subsequently evaluated across eighteen failure scenarios in Chapter~8. As such, the problem formulation in Chapter~4 and the findings in Section~8.4 represent two sides of the same coin: the latter provides the former with empirical substantiation, which in turn serves as a theoretical foundation for the choice of coverage redundancy in Section~6.8.3. This distinction is elaborated further in Section~\ref{sec:ch9_limits}.

Another inconsistency in the problem formulation concerns the throughput guarantee in Section~4.1.2. Specifically, Chapter~4 only considers the narrow aisle's clearance constraint in terms of the geometric obstruction $W_{\text{aisle}} < 2 \cdot D_{\text{safe}} + \epsilon$ in Section~4.1.2.1 while omitting the impact that the \texttt{owned\_regions} arrangement in \texttt{config.py} can have on the parallel efficiency once the fleet size increases. Section~8.2 documents this interaction by reporting a reduction in parallel efficiency from $99.4\,\%$ at $N=2$ to $90.3\,\%$ at $N=3$ (Eq.~\eqref{eq:parallel_efficiency}). The reason for this drop stems directly from the three pairwise region overlaps between \texttt{robot1}, \texttt{robot2}, and \texttt{robot3} that only become simultaneously active once the third robot is added to the \texttt{config.py} formation, thereby reducing the overall parallel efficiency by 9.1 percentage points. The formulation in Chapter~4 does not account for this effect due to the independence assumption between the corridor contention and region assignment policies; essentially, Section~8.2 identifies a flaw in the original problem formulation. One possible next step in this line of research is elaborated in Section~\ref{sec:ch9_future}.

Overall, the progression from Chapter~4 forward represents a confirmation of the problem formulation's initial assumptions rather than a refutation: the formulation only claimed a weaker coverage guarantee, and the eighteen-run campaign did not report any outcomes contradicting it. The one shortcoming in the initial formulation concerns the failure to consider the interaction between the \texttt{owned\_regions} structure and fleet size, which manifested in the reduced parallel efficiency in Section~8.2. What the original formulation failed to consider was the possibility of a similar shortcoming in the verbal coverage resilience claims in Section~6.8.3, whereas Section~\ref{sec:ch9_limits} examines the option of supplementing them with a formal proof. Aside from these theoretical considerations, Section~\ref{sec:ch9_engineering} focuses on a different aspect of building and iterating on this project: the lessons learned throughout the process.

\section{Reflections on Implementation-Phase Engineering Risk\label{sec:ch9_engineering}}

Unlike Chapter~7, which documents the system's engineering surface as it was eventually realized (a particular set of resolved package layouts, a particular deterministic launch graph, a particular single-writer TF tree), this chapter contains debugging records of design decisions that were changed mid-implementation due to specific failures. A run-time log of dozens individual incidents of this class has been recorded for the duration of development, covering rendering, localization, task allocation, and lower-level Python utilities. The present section is not organized as a chronological change-log, because this would involve considerable repetition of superficial details while obscuring whatever generic lessons had been learned. The incidents described below have been grouped according to five general classes of hazard. These classes are each illustrated by a particular case in this chapter, and each has been previously discussed in the broader robotics or distributed systems literature.

\subsection{Simulation Fidelity Under Virtualized Rendering and Physics Hosts}

Two of the earliest and most frustrating failures experienced by the development team were not related to the coordination algorithms described in Chapters~5 or~6, but were instead imposed by the host on which the simulation was running. RViz2's global costmap panel would render a completely empty image when launched under WSL2 with NVIDIA's graphics acceleration enabled, due to a shader compiler error in the Mesa Gallium D3D12 implementation that sits between the Linux and Windows kernels; the only workaround was for the team to revert to CPU-based rendering via \texttt{LIBGL\_ALWAYS\_SOFTWARE}, which significantly impacts the performance of RViz2. A separate, but related, issue affected the physics simulation: the TurtleBot3 Waffle's caster wheel would sink approximately one centimeter into the ground on Gazebo launch, rotating the robot by $-0.01\,\text{rad}$ around the X-axis and causing its 2D LiDAR to face upward toward the shelves of the warehouse environment, resulting in all subsequent scans being malformed. Neither of these issues are related to the coordination algorithm discussed in this paper, and their effects would not have been predicted by an examination of Chapter~5 alone. Both originate in the graphics and physics translation layers beneath the simulated robot body.

The common theme between these two incidents is that a virtualized and translated software environment introduces a category of errors orthogonal to the domain of the application. The benchmarking literature on CUDA applications under virtualization offers several empirical data points describing similar performance penalties and irregularities that manifest despite a fixed hardware and algorithm stack \cite{belkhiri2024analyzing}. The two issues described in this section are specific instances of this generic concern: the Gazebo Harmonic physics engine and the RViz2 Ogre2 renderer are each tuned to perform well on "native" Linux systems, but neither is tested against WSL2-specific translation layers, leaving responsibility for discovering such issues to the user who runs the simulation. This is the same technical premise that motivates Section~8.1's restriction on fleet size: scaling down the number of robots launched at once would not be sufficient to avoid these issues if they occurred.

\subsection{Namespace and Transform-Tree Isolation as a Distributed-Systems Consistency Problem}

A second broad category of failures originated in the interactions between three equivalent robot stacks running within a single ROS 2 graph. Early testing employed a Xacro macro to pass a common namespace argument to each robot description, while also attempting to install a \texttt{robot\_state\_publisher} \texttt{frame\_prefix} parameter at the same level to avoid TF tree collisions. However, the combination of these two naming sources caused the frame names to be doubled, which caused Nav2 to fail whenever it attempted to find a transform with a name that did not match the expected robot-specific root. A similar class of failures affected the static portion of the TF frame tree: because static transforms use a Transient Local durability policy by default, a relay node that subscribed to \texttt{robot\_state\_publisher} after the initial static transform had been published would miss the message entirely and cause intermittent TF tree discontinuities with no accompanying error message. Finally, a higher-level issue affected \texttt{bt\_navigator}: when the second robot's Nav2 stack was brought online, the \texttt{bt\_navigator} component immediately failed due to a duplicate registration of the \texttt{ComputePathToPose} behavior-tree node.

The common theme between these three issues is that two subsystems attempted to claim ownership over the same global resource without awareness of one another. There is nothing intrinsically wrong with any of these three subsystems individually; rather, all three incidents originate in the distributed systems properties of ROS 2: multiple nodes incorrectly assuming that they are the sole writers for a particular transform or topic. This is not specific to the solution described in this paper: several recent works on multi-robot coordination address ROS 2's limitations in communication isolation as a critical issue that requires a comparative analysis of alternative strategies \cite{naury2025communication}. The three incidents described in this section were addressed in the implementation by using a combination of techniques that fall under the broad category of avoiding namespace collisions (Section~7.3). The URDF link names were adjusted to avoid the implicit namespace doubling (Section~7.3.1), a strict authority model was introduced for transform publication (Section~7.3.2), and the two truly global topics (\texttt{/add\_waypoints} and \texttt{/side\_claims}) were moved out of the robot-local namespaces entirely (Section~7.3.3). However, each of these remedies was developed as a response to an individual incident, which highlights the general challenge of preemptively designing against namespace collisions.

\subsection{Perceptual Aliasing and Drift in Geometrically Repetitive Industrial Environments}

The third class of problems was caused by an unusual geometrical arrangement rather than a particular problem in the software stack: the rows of shelving units in the warehouse, which were identical within planar transformations, led to perceptual aliasing in AMCL, which caused its covariance to exceed $13\,\text{m}$ before being able to relocalize. The reason for this was that 2D LiDAR scans provided much stronger constraints on the pose of the robot along the aisles than on its position along the direction of the aisle, and so errors in the wheel encoders were not corrected until the robot reached a distinguishing landmark such as the corner or end of an aisle. The same generic cause affected the Cartographer's offline loop closure detection in Section~7.7.2, but this was fixed by adjusting thresholds for loop closure scores, not the robotic sensors, as the reference map only has to be built once.

The perceptual aliasing problem causing IMU drift in visual SLAM is well-known in the literature for its tendency to appear in loop closure failures in repetitive industrial environments \cite{kim2025query}. The EKF fusion algorithm of the odometry and IMU yaw-rate information, described in Section~5.4.5, and a practical mask policy, described in Section~7.5.1, fixes the along-aisle drift estimates but not the jump discontinuities in the reference frame, which were addressed by the decrease in loop closure thresholds rather than changes to the robot's sensors, as they only have to be built once. Similar considerations apply to the localization stack's use of loop closures in future localization passes in general, though to a lesser extent.

\subsection{Compounding Safety Margins in Layered Costmap Architectures}

A fourth class of incidents was concerned with the robot's safety margins relative to obstacles: in particular, cases where the theoretical and practical margins were not the same. The simplest case was a topic subscription error: the local costmap's obstacle-layer configuration pointed to a relative \texttt{scan} topic instead of an absolute one, leading to the layer plugin silently subscribing to an invalid topic; since the node itself existed in a per-robot namespace, it produced no error messages, only behaving as if the LiDAR scans were always empty. Two other issues were related to the local costmap's safety margins being larger than the ones used by the planning stack, with the robot frequently attempting to drive through previously unseen interior spaces of shelves or other obstacles. The first time this occurred, the costmap's unknown space was treated as traversable by default, leading to the robot driving straight through the center of a shelf during the first few passes; a similar issue was discovered on later shelves, but it stemmed not from a misconfiguration but from a single-axis retraction policy: a waypoint near the middle of a steel structural column could not be retracted far enough to avoid the column's safety zone if the normal retraction direction was along the axis of the shelf, since the column was placed on the cross-aisle axis and was not affected by motion along that axis.

The three issues, unrelated as they seemed at the time, had one major factor in common: an interaction between several parameters that only manifested when taken together as a system. The distance offset, robot radius, obstacle inflation radius, and retraction direction together defined a set of implicit safety margins that a layered costmap setup like the one described in Section~5.4.2 \cite{macenski2023survey} did not account for individually. Multiple recent works on scenario-based validation of mobile robot systems highlight this fact, specifically, how minimum safe separation between obstacles and robot chassis needs to be defined in terms of stopping distance, robot rotation radius, and obstacle geometry jointly, rather than picking one or two design factors and trying to define safety margins in terms of them \cite{pasch2026robovast}. Both the lateral-shifting and widened-offset fixes adopted to avoid the steel columns fall into this category of system-level safety margin considerations. The same principle guided the parametric structural avoidance for the two large shelf faces pressed against the steel structural columns, formalized later as a fixed $13.50\,\text{m}$ target coordinate in Section~5.3.3. None of the fixes applied universally to the navigation stack, however, since none of them addressed the possibility of a similar set of parameters interacting in a similar way elsewhere in the warehouse.

\subsection{Silent Logical Faults in Decentralized Heuristic Control Code}

A fifth class of issues came from situations where the robot continued executing a plan even when it was wrong. In one case, a Python scoping quirk in an early attempt at a target-selection helper function caused an \texttt{UnboundLocalError} condition to terminate the decision thread whenever the selector attempted to proceed to the next shelf, leaving the robot stuck at whatever side it had initially scanned. A second, unrelated bug came from using a half-plane check rather than a conic section in one stage of the target approach direction selection logic; using this check, a shelf lying almost due east but slightly north of the robot in absolute coordinates would be treated as a northern shelf, leading to a full sweep rotation before the robot could realize it was actually facing east. A third bug did not have a wrong decision as its root cause but an infinite loop in the decision-making code itself: the internal goal-checker tolerance in Nav2 and the state machine's distance check were off by several centimeters, causing the robot to repeatedly declare the same goal reached, cancel it, and replace it with another one. In a similar vein, the state machine's reaction to a failed navigation goal was to return to the decision-making loop immediately rather than waiting for a cooldown period, causing a deluge of claim and retry messages to saturate the DDS transport and cause the single-threaded executor to ignore incoming failure notifications from other robots.

Not one of these four issues stemmed from a problem in the robot hardware or a configuration error somewhere in the robotics stack. What united them was a potential for a logical flaw in a decentralized decision-making system to produce silent failures, where a robot would continue executing the plan even when it was no longer applicable, either due to a particular geometric arrangement of objects or due to a race condition in the timing of events. Several recent works on decentralized swarm control and testing begin to draw attention to such silent logical flaws as a class, specifically, the sheer volume of parameter-space combinations a swarm control program can explore rendering traditional unit and integration testing insufficient, and suggesting fuzzing as an alternative approach \cite{zhou2025rsfuzz}. Neither of these four issues were discovered through such a method, however; each was found through a robot acting incorrectly at some point during a manual inspection run, which is less efficient than the fuzzing approach advocated for in the swarm testing literature.

Read together, the five classes of issues described above have more in common with the supporting literature outside this thesis: similar discussions concern virtualization performance and design patterns, containerization security, perceptual aliasing, layered costmap validation, and swarm logic testing. None of these issues were anticipated by the formal models of Chapters~5 and~6, and none of the solutions involved revisiting those models; rather, each of the five had a resolution at the engineering level of Chapter~7. This division of labor is convenient, but it is also worth noting it as a limitation in and of itself; if a formal model only ever interacts with the layer below it, rather than accounting for it, then it is a model of something that does not yet exist. Section~\ref{sec:ch9_limits} returns to this point in more detail.

\section{Limitations of the Present Work\label{sec:ch9_limits}}

The prior two sections framed what our Chapter~8 results did and did not establish. This section clarifies the distinction, specifying precisely where the present work fails to meet the theoretical expectations of our own designs.

The fault model of Chapter~6 is non-Byzantine as stated in Section~6.1.1 -- a failed robot may stop responding, but will not send misleading state reports. Nothing in the claim protocol, heartbeat, or reallocation process was designed to handle an agent that continues reporting health but exhibits physically different behaviors. No such protections were built into any of the claim, heartbeat, or reallocation mechanisms since, for the target domain of one company operating their own fleet over a private network, there was no evident value in designing against adversarial take-over. The resilience bound of Eq.~\eqref{eq:fleet_survival_boundary} carries the same caveat and, as noted in Section~\ref{sec:ch9_revisit}, was instead an empirically-observed limit rather than a formal assertion. None of the system invariants were formally verified in a model checker, nor were any of the safety- or liveness-critical properties of the claim-overwrite protocol of Section~5.2.5 subjected to a mathematical proof -- only an eighteen-test run deadlock detection audit.

Scalability sits as a second-order limitation, since all of the throughput and resilience figures of Chapter~8 were gathered on no more than three agents at once. The $\mathcal{O}(N^2)$ subscription overhead of Eq.~\eqref{eq:network_traffic_scale} was not benchmarked beyond the three-unit edge, but the parallel efficiency curve of Table~\ref{tab:nominal_tat_scaling} already drops by an order of magnitude between $N=2$ and $N=3$. Is this curve approaching $N=5$ as it nears $N=10$, or has the drop-off accelerated beyond the bend-point? This study cannot say, since none of the metrics of Chapter~8 were rebenchmarked beyond the three-unit edge. Similarly, although the cascading-failure kill-switch of Section~6.6.3 was written to handle a second agent loss while the first was reallocating, no test runs in Chapter~8 actually triggered two failures within a single mission. The nearest approximation of a concurrent two-agent failure was the double baseline scenario of Section~8.1, which killed both robots at $t \approx 0$ before any tasks had been claimed. This tested the survivor math of Eq.~\eqref{eq:survivor_set_redefinition} but not the kill-switch recovery protocols.

A third class of limitations concerns the space of failure modes to which the system was exposed. For example, all fifteen faulty-to-reallocation cases in Table~\ref{tab:ftl_by_condition} used the explicit self-declaration channel. The heartbeat-timeout logic of Eq.~\eqref{eq:heartbeat_timeout_formula}, extensively discussed in Section~6.1.2, was not even triggered in any of the eighteen trials because there was simply no such thing as a silent failure to declare. An analogous point can be made for the perception stack: out of 7278 total scans processed, not one necessitated the XGBoost fallback of Section~6.7.1, and the only potential failure mode in the shared preprocessing pipeline (Section~6.7.2) was a theoretical concern rather than an empirical reality. Both issues were acknowledged in Section~8.7; briefly reiterating them here is useful to emphasize that these were not "obvious" limitations but rather "orthogonal" design choices unproven by the absence of the circumstances they were designed to handle.

A fourth class of limitations concerns the system's ability to diagnose itself. The choice to make the blacklisting events occur at the shelf-side level rather than per-section (as discussed in detail in Section~6.5.3), introduced a diagnostic overhead summarized in Section~8.6: namely, that the logs of Run~\#13 could only diagnose one out of the three simultaneous waypoint losses as happening due to a particular mechanism. It bears repeating here that it was not a failure of post-hoc analysis but rather a design limitation: the claim manager and task coordinator simply did not record the information necessary to distinguish between the concurrent waypoint losses beyond the first.

Finally, a brief note on the generalizability of the current findings. All results of Chapter~8, and by extension all hazard classifications of Section~\ref{sec:ch9_engineering}, were obtained in a single WSL2 virtual machine hosting a Gazebo Harmonic simulation. None of the claims made in this paper have been tested on an actual TurtleBot3 hardware, and the entire analysis conducted here is subject to the "reality gap" common to robotic simulation. The most recent literature on robot simulation emphasizes that modern perception stacks and physics engines implement domain randomization and/or real-to-sim calibration to reduce this reality gap, and that sim-to-real transfer requires specialized training and/or domain adaptation \cite{aljalbout2026reality}. The current work did not employ any such domain adaptation due to the analysis being conducted within a single target domain (single simulated fleet), but this caveat is noted here for completeness. In short, all numeric claims and empirical observations of Chapter~8 are made within the context of a simulated fleet of robots, not physical ones.

None of the limitations listed above actually vitiate any of the contributions listed in Section~\ref{sec:ch9_synthesis} -- they all represent ways in which this work could, in principle, be expanded rather than directions it was proven wrong in. Section~\ref{sec:ch9_future} tackles each of these limitations in turn, proposing specific follow-up experiments.

\section{Future Work\label{sec:ch9_future}}

The five limitations of Section~\ref{sec:ch9_limits} do not each call for an isolated line of future research. Grouped together, they cleanly fall across the four conceptual layers of the five-layer architecture of Section~6.8.1 -- namely, sensor, kinematic, coordination, and perception. Each was actually anticipated in the relevant synthesis section, although none were fully worked out beyond articulating the premise. The next few subsections do not follow the limitations section in turn, but rather approach future work from the vantage point of domain layers rather than outstanding issues -- a single innovation, such as the replacement of the claim-overwrite rule of Eq.~\eqref{eq:asymmetric_precedence} with a formally verified liveness proof, may well address multiple limitations at once.

\subsection{Coordination and Allocation: From Heuristic Sweep to Verified Liveness}

Table~\ref{tab:lit_review_comparison} actually encapsulates the fundamental limitation of the coordination layer -- that a deterministic, nearly trivial cardinal sweep was selected in favor of a richer but more complex DARP-style approach to reallocating regions under dynamically varying constraints \cite{sharma2026darp}. Section~\ref{sec:ch9_revisit} actually assigned a specific metric to the limitations of this design choice -- recall that the efficiency gain between $99.4\,\%$ at $N=2$ and $90.3\,\%$ at $N=3$ (Eq.~\eqref{eq:parallel_efficiency}) cannot be attributed to \texttt{owned\_regions} alone. The reason three pairwise overlaps were only seen when the fleet size grew from two to three agents was that the \texttt{owned\_regions} partition had to be fixed at deployment rather than dynamically shrunk as the surviving fleet did. A small change to \texttt{config.py}, then, would decouple fleet size from partition allocation, letting future studies cleanly separate the impact of topo-mutex effects on deadlocks from mere subscription overhead. This work would not need to be repeated at higher concurrency levels -- the three-unit edge explored in this study would suffice to observe the bend-point of the curve in Eq.~\eqref{eq:parallel_efficiency}.

More interestingly, this study's limitations extend to mathematical verifiability rather than mere performance tuning. The \texttt{ClaimManager} of Section~5.2.5 only needed eighteen deadlock-free runs rather than a general liveness proof, with this distinction highlighted in Section~\ref{sec:ch9_limits}. The recent articles about decentralized navigation deal with the same problem, but from the opposite side, creating "barrier certificate" which guarantee that deadlock will not happen in the system, for any set of agents, while for each individual agent they do not provide any safety guarantees \cite{chandra2025deadlock}. Applying the similar technique to the corridor-lease predicate described by the Eq.~\eqref{eq:spatial_availability_check} would not change the runtime logic of the \texttt{ClaimManager} but would significantly extend the reach of the conclusions made in this section.

Scalability actually presents a set of constraints that go well beyond the target domain of this work. Nothing in this study actually measured the $\mathcal{O}(N^2)$ subscription overhead of Eq.~\eqref{eq:network_traffic_scale} beyond $N=3$ but industrial scale competitors to Amazon's DeepFleet routinely exceed that concurrency by several orders of magnitude. These competitors do not attempt to publish individual status updates to every other agent in the fleet -- rather, the DeepFleet approach trains foundation models on logs of fleet behavior, letting emergent patterns dictate coordination rather than relying on deterministic pairwise subscriptions \cite{agaskar2025deepfleet}. This study does not suggest that a three-robot fleet needs such a foundation model -- but the limits of the peer-to-peer subscription model actually used in this project are now evident between the three agents this study benchmarks and the hundreds of thousands supported by industrial alternatives.

Finally, the non-Byzantine fault assumption of Section~6.1.1 was deliberately conservative but still a design limitation. Recent literature on blockchain-driven swarm autonomy actually develops consensus mechanisms that tolerate arbitrary faults within strict computational budgets -- notably, within the constraints of physical hardware rather than cloud-scale distributed computing \cite{wang2025byzantineswarm}. How these mechanisms compare to the reallocation window of Table~\ref{tab:ftl_by_condition} is an open question, but it is a much more approachable engineering challenge than formal verification would be.

\subsection{Localization and Fault Prediction: Beyond the Binary Heartbeat}

The heartbeat-timeout channel of Eq.~\eqref{eq:heartbeat_timeout_formula} did not trigger at all across eighteen runs, a fact which Section~\ref{sec:ch9_limits} rightfully reads as an unexploited capability rather than a demonstrated limitation. Part of the explanation for this lies in design; the timeout channel is a single-threshold check on message absence, and thus ignores any potential pattern of precursors which might have indicated an impending failure before the fifth consecutive second of silence. A recent approach to multi-robot fault diagnosis touches on a similar point in theoretical terms, noting that memoryless state classifiers are fundamentally unable to encode fault-specific patterns of preceding observations, and suggesting a recurrent architecture trained on the same generalized behavioral features as an alternative \cite{couceiro2026learning}. Noting the same problem at the level of the five-second timeout does not render a similar redesign mandatory, but layering such a model in parallel to the existing channels would allow the robots to react to early signs of failure with less urgency than the binary backstop requires.

The same line of reasoning would also apply to a deeper layer within the localization stack: the covariance-triggered recovery of Section~6.2.1. The $\sigma_{\text{relax}} = 0.50\,\text{m}$ threshold is a compromise between the false-positive spin rate and the collision-probability bound of Eq.~\eqref{eq:collision_prob_relax}, and as such it embodies a reactive philosophy -- waiting for the covariance to bloom before taking action. A natural extension of the previous point would be to inquire into the possibility of designing an analogous sequential model, trained on the AMCL covariance trace, that could anticipate the imminent need for covariance resetting, thereby reducing the $54\,\%$ inflation risk which the relaxed threshold allows.

None of this addresses the reality-vs-simulation concern raised in Section~\ref{sec:ch9_limits}, and no number of elaborately instrumented tests can substitute for hardware-level trials. A recent decentralized warehouse platform utilizing the same ROS~2/DDS stack provides concrete comparisons for the kinds of physical trials this thesis is limited to: it achieved a $94.2\,\%$ mapping accuracy against ground truth, and recovered from localization failure $32\,\%$ faster than its centralized counterpart in a dual-core Raspberry Pi testbed \cite{gaadhe2026collective}. It is instructive to consider what these figures might imply for this thesis's more tightly coupled system, but they are not conclusive evidence either that the sub-six-millisecond FTL threshold of the EKF localization or the $0.043\,\text{m}$ correction radius of its fault-tolerance layer would survive the transition to physical hardware.

\subsection{Perception and Analytical Redundancy: Testing the Untested Fallback}

Section~8.5 notes the complete absence of reactions from the XGBoost fallback during the 7278 scans of the campaign, and takes care to qualify this as an absence of evidence rather than evidence of capability. A recent survey of safety-critical verification methods for machine learning perception highlights the same concern and elaborates it into a five-part structure: threat modeling, requirement formulation, failure detection, response, and evaluation. Failure response mechanisms in particular are reported on overwhelmingly as theoretical concerns without the supporting fault-injection testing which would demonstrate that a given reaction channel activates and behaves as expected when its design conditions are met \cite{ferreira2025safety}. This is precisely the situation that Section~6.7.1 finds itself in with regard to the analytical redundancy of its perception stack.

A practical campaign along the lines of the survey would not have to rely on inevitable equipment failures to accumulate such evidence. The intentional corruption of some element of the 125-dimensional PCA-projected feature vector prior to its injection into Eq.~\eqref{eq:svm_decision_function}'s SVM decision function, or a forced division by zero during the standardization step shared by the pipeline in Section~6.7.2, would be sufficient to obtain meaningful metrics on the fallback latency. Instead of postulating, as Table~\ref{tab:edge_classifier_comparison} does, on the relative qualities of two classifier architectures across two dimensions at once, a similarly situated campaign could attempt this sort of evaluation on a narrower front -- for instance, on whether the analytical-redundancy layer's reported accuracy improvements translate into execution speed improvements under the $5.0\,\text{ms}$ WCET budget of Eq.~\eqref{eq:fallback_processing_time}.

\subsection{Energy-Aware Allocation and Deployment Scalability}

The battery-proportional allocation of Eq.~\eqref{eq:battery_proportional_ratio} is a one-time decision made with a single known failure in mind rather than an ongoing reallocation procedure, a choice which places it at odds with the continuous charge-preserving optimization of the state of the art \cite{notomista2022resilient,djenadi2022energyaware}. The fail-stop paradigm of Eq.~\eqref{eq:absolute_failure_threshold} was justified at the level of individual robots in Section~4.2.4.2 due to the docking station's potential to create narrow-aisle deadlocks, but a recent scheduling framework for multi-robot systems under energy constraints makes an alternative proposal. Instead of treating charging as an event orthogonal to task allocation, it incorporates the robots' ability to bid on charging slots in a joint online auction and thus smooths short-term fluctuations in the battery state \cite{dilefeld2025scheduling}. This allows the robots to make decisions with an eye toward both task and power budgeting without requiring either to be formulated as a global optimization problem. The same principle might be applied here to attempt an alternative to the two extremes mentioned in the question: without rewriting the fail-stop allocation logic of Chapter~8, a separate campaign using this scheduling framework as a baseline might attempt to answer whether the proactive allocation model changes the distribution of the coverage-loss curves described in Table~\ref{tab:injection_timing_summary} beyond their mean.

Taken together, these points do not constitute a singular experiment which could be tried 'next', but rather a set of conditions which must be tried elsewhere before any one of them can be confidently reiterated here. The physical validation which the WSL2/Gazebo model used for Chapter~8 has been subjected to is sorely lacking, and the best evidence of this method's ability to substitute for real hardware comes in the form of similar benchmarks for a different decentralized DDS-powered architecture \cite{gaadhe2026collective}.

\section{Concluding Remarks\label{sec:ch9_closing}}

Chapter~4 established this thesis's contributions to the warehouse-inspection problem in the form of a limited workspace, a set of 142 targets to visit, and two claims which neither overstate the capabilities of a decentralized multi-robot system nor shrink them unrealistically. Chapters 5 through 8 then spent the following pages constructing a system which might fulfill these requirements, and subsequently measured, across eighteen runs, how far a working instance of it would fall short against an actual claim manager, an actual particle filter, and an actual physics engine running in a virtualized host. This campaign's findings are, in a sense, the most honest the thesis can offer: the coordination layer scales almost linearly with the number of robots at the level of two, and drops by around ten percent at three due to a measurable, describable interaction which has nothing to do with the mutex being defective in principle. The fault-tolerance layer reroutes the failed robot's tasks in single-digit milliseconds every time it is tested, but two of its three channels remain unexploited and unused across the campaign, awaiting a failure scenario which never manifests. All of these findings together constitute the most thorough evaluation of a masterless three-robot fleet this venue can offer. Whether they can be expanded into a similarly comprehensive assessment of a four- or five-robot fleet, or the behaviors of a rogue robot or a robot with physical wheel slippage, is left as an exercise for the next occupant of this venue as well.
