\chapter{Related Work and Literature Review\label{cha:chapter3}}

The theoretical and engineering contributions of this thesis build upon several disparate research areas, each of which involves a significant degree of heterogeneity in terminology, performance benchmarks, and design priorities. These areas include multi-robot task allocation, decentralized spatial coordination, fault-tolerant fleet management, probabilistic localization recovery, and edge-deployed visual inspection. This chapter provides an overview of the relevant literature for each of these areas, with a particular emphasis on contrasting the design choices made in this thesis to alternative approaches that have been validated in comparable problem domains. In cases where another approach offers demonstrable advantages over the one used in this thesis, these are presented as opportunities for future improvement rather than outright shortcomings, since the choices made in this work were necessarily driven by the need to achieve reasonable performance in a particular deployment envelope (three homogeneous ground robots, a warehouse domain with fixed geometry, and a CPU-bound WSL2 sandbox; cf. Section~7.2.3). Where applicable, the findings of this chapter are used to motivate specific contributions in Chapters~5--7.

\section{Multi-Robot Task Allocation and Coverage Planning in Warehouse Logistics}

The literature on Multi-Robot Task Allocation (MRTA) since the foundational taxonomy by Gerkey and Mataric is sprawling and difficult to navigate, with recent surveys seeking to impose order by distinguishing market-based, behavior-based, and optimization-based approaches, among others. Chakraa et al.\ highlight that while optimization-based methods are theoretically most powerful, they tend to ``scale poorly with an increase in the number of tasks and robots,'' especially beyond the limits of mixed-integer solvers' capabilities \cite{chakraa2023optimization}. Quinton et al.\ take a narrower focus, comparing various auction mechanisms for patrolling, exploration, and pick-and-delivery scenarios, and noting that ``most auction mechanisms assume a communication infrastructure that is significantly more reliable than likely to be found in [a warehouse filled with steel shelves]'' \cite{quinton2023market}. Neither survey settles the question of what MRTA approach is most suitable for a particular domain; instead, they all suggest that such a choice is necessarily tied to the balance between the communication overhead, solution quality, and task set dynamics.

With regard to warehouse applications specifically, Bolu and Kor\c{c}ak describe an adaptive planning approach for a smart warehouse, where priority weights for each task are adjusted dynamically based on demand fluctuations \cite{bolu2021adaptive}. This approach appears to be driven primarily by the need to balance the robot fleet, which is orthogonal to the problem domain of this thesis, since the 142-waypoint inspection set is effectively static (the closest analogy to a ``demand fluctuation'' is an emergency inspection added by the user). By contrast, Agrawal et al.\ propose a tightly coupled task-allocation and navigation stack for warehouse management, DC-MRTA, which explicitly considers traffic-aware allocation and congestion costs \cite{agrawal2022dcmrta}. This is perhaps the only prior work to directly address the challenge of task allocation as a function of fleet state, and the closest to the approach described in Section~5.1. The allocation method used in this thesis to support the waypoint delivery mechanism avoids the issue of ``wrong-way'' motions altogether by relying on the same-side-lock and same-item heuristics described in Section~5.3, whereas DC-MRTA and similar approaches tackle it at the level of the task allocation.

The literature on region-based partitioning methods is, similarly to MRTA, challenging to navigate due to the sheer diversity of relevant publications. Sharma et al.\ provide a brief overview of partitioning methods, including their Divide and Rule (DARP) algorithm, which divides the workspace into robot-specific subregions before launching an intra-cell planner for each robot, and a hybrid DARP-and-A* approach, which allows for dynamic reallocation when environment conditions change \cite{sharma2026darp}. The static partitioning approach of DARP appears to be close in spirit to the \texttt{owned\_regions} concept described in this thesis, although the cardinal-direction sweep and same-side-lock heuristic described in Section~5.3 are much lighter on computation and have different performance characteristics. Specifically, the dynamic reallocation mechanism described by Sharma et al.\ is comparatively more flexible but also more computationally demanding, which is at odds with the constraints of a WSL2 sandbox. Moreover, the decision function that underlies the cardinal-direction sweep is deterministic and nearly costless to evaluate, affording consistency between independent instances of the planner that would be important for coordinating multi-robot traffic; as discussed in Section~5.2, idempotent and repeatable decisions are crucial for making progress under the corridor-mutex protocol.

\section{Decentralized Spatial Mutual Exclusion and Deadlock Avoidance in Confined Multi-Robot Workspaces}

Deadlock in multi-robot systems has seen a more systematic treatment in recent works than the case-by-case heuristics of the early literature. Grover et al.\ characterize conditions under which it occurs in reactive multi-robot navigation, formalizing necessary circumstances and cataloguing detection and recovery approaches from the collision avoidance literature \cite{grover2023deadlock}. Their taxonomy serves as a helpful reference point for the corridor-lease mechanism of Section~5.2.3: the claims made there are primarily preventive rather than detective-and-recover, since they aim to prevent the formation of mutual deadlock ``from the inside,'' i.e., prevent two robots from starting motion along the same narrow aisle from opposing directions in the first place.

Industrial AGV research offers the closest comparison to the corridor-lease mechanism described in Section~5.2.3. Pratissoli et al.\ propose a hierarchical traffic management method for multi-AGV systems which utilizes reservations of guide-path segments ahead of each vehicle and releases them once the vehicle has physically cleared the space, specifically to prevent deadlocks on narrow aisles \cite{pratissoli2024hierarchical}. He et al.\ take a resource-allocation-graph approach to the same problem, verifying liveness properties of a closed guide-path system with a formally specified reservation policy \cite{he2023deadlock}. Both works operate under the implicit coordination assumption of a centralized, globally visible guide-path graph managed by a supervisory controller program: the \texttt{ClaimManager} of Section~5.2 implements a similar exclusion property to these works by making peer-to-peer broadcasts of leases held on each narrow aisle, with a six-second timeout replacing the graph-theoretic reservations, while sacrificing deadlock-detection guarantees that would require a similarly global view of the system state. The relative simplicity of the lease-based management algorithm comes at the cost of the inability to formally specify system-wide liveness properties and prevent deadlocks, which are instead handled heuristically using an empirical campaign over eighteen different scenarios in Chapter~8.

While similar to the token-passing mutual exclusion algorithms of distributed systems, the lease-based mechanism described in Section~5.2.3 draws more comparisons to routing policies for automated guided vehicles. Parihar and Chakraborty implement a token-based k-mutual exclusion scheme for UAVs operating over a lossy FANET communication graph: by limiting the number of UAVs that can simultaneously claim a shared resource and passing a token between them over the unreliable network, they show that their system maintains safe traffic separation despite intermittent link failures \cite{parihar2022token}. The corridor claims described in this work are most similar to such token-passing schemes in their use of exclusive ownership of narrow-conduit resources to prevent deadlocks, but differ in their Last-Come-First-Served overwrite behavior. In case of packet loss, a failed token renewal would cause permanent deadlock for the UAVs involved, requiring expensive network intervention to recover, whereas a similar LCFS-style failure in the claims mechanism would only cause one robot to release and subsequently reacquire ownership of the narrow aisle. The latter behavior was explicitly selected, in Section~5.2.4, as a way to avoid having to implement order-dependence guarantees beyond those provided by the underlying DDS middleware, which is unable to provide sequential consistency between distributed nodes. Like the claims of Section~5.2.3, the FANET token-passing scheme of Parihar and Chakraborty has to make similar concessions to practical reliability at the cost of formal verification: in the eighteenth scenario of Chapter~8, a single failed claim renewal causes two robots to simultaneously attempt to acquire the same corridor, which is resolved through an empirical deadlock detection scheme (Figure~8.7). Deadlock freedom is not formally guaranteed or proven, being instead validated through the campaign of eighteen scenarios described in that section. This trade-off in reasoning about system state allows for significant reductions in computational or communication overhead, which is particularly crucial when dealing with the unreliable connectivity of industrial wireless networks operating ROS 2's peer-to-peer messaging topology. This topology's bandwidth constraints impose considerable limitations on DDS discovery and communication graphs, such that real-time traffic safety applications are prone to intermittent connectivity failures and subsequent cascading network disruptions due to RTPS IP fragmentation and ACKNACK retransmission timeouts \cite{lee2025optimizing}. Under such conditions, token-passing or sequentially-consistent distributed mutual exclusion algorithms suffer considerable overhead from failed communication attempts, whereas the LCFS-style concurrency control of the claims mechanism avoids them by relying exclusively on the delivery of local claims messages. Thus, while the lack of global view of the system state required to implement such a mechanism limits the ability to formally verify and prevent deadlocks, it alleviates the overhead of transactional synchronization and prevents such failures from occurring due to intermittent packet loss.

\section{Failure Taxonomies and Fault-Tolerant Coordination in Decentralized Fleets}

The fail-stop and fail-silent distinction introduced in Section~6.1.1 is far from unique to this thesis; it forms a part of the ongoing discussion about the amount of information a robot swarm can extract about another robot's internal state from the latter's observable behavior. Lee et al.\ develop a data-driven pipeline for precisely this purpose, extracting behavioral metrics from a subject robot's motion and detecting faults that adhere to the fail-silent hypothesis to identify deviations from the expected behavior \cite{lee2022datadriven}. Bossens et al.\ place the same concept in the context of general resilient robotics, suggesting that decentralized control, change-detection mechanisms, and online adaptation capabilities fall into three separate categories, which existing solutions rarely combine \cite{bossens2022resilient}. By that metric, the fleet in this thesis is even more limited, only featuring decentralized control and very limited change-detection means – the explicit self-reporting of faults and a periodic heartbeat ping from each robot. The reason for this limitation is explained in Section~6.1.1 – the non-Byzantine nature of faults in this thesis rules out behaviors like Lee et al.\ suggest, while adding an additional fault-detection mechanism to a WSL2 process on a strained CPU would be hard to justify.

On the topic of reallocation, it is unsurprising to find similar concepts in the literature, though rarely in sufficient detail. Mayya et al.\ frame the problem in terms of heterogenous multi-robot task allocation, describing an online algorithm for dynamically apportioning rewards to maximize the expected utility of a robot team, given the estimated capabilities of all robots in the system \cite{mayya2021resilient}. The proportional battery-based allocation described in Section~6.6 is clearly more limited in scope, only applying to robots that have been positively identified as failed, with the reward vector recalculated only once. A more geometric approach to the same problem is found in Gong et al.'s work on mobile clusters, which the author has already cited in Section~4.1.4 – the proportional allocation of a failed robot's Boustrophedon partition to its neighbors is also performed iteratively and locally, by propagating a series of updates through the cluster's communication graph \cite{gong2024resilient}. The main difference between the approaches is that the local recomputations happen on individual segments of the workspace, rather than the entire set of unprocessed items, which is feasible due to the small size of the fleet in this thesis (a three-robot team). The same approach would be much less useful if applied to the multi-robot systems mentioned above.

A finding that unifies almost all works on fault-tolerant robotics mentioned in this section, and is borne out by the results of Section~8.3, is that decentralized control typically comes at the price of increased bandwidth overhead. The $\mathcal{O}(N^2)$ scaling of Section~6.1.3 is in fact a particular case of this trend, which applies to any system that replicates status information over a distributed network of observers rather than a shared channel.

\section{AMCL-Based Localization Recovery and the Kidnapped-Robot Problem}

Adaptive Monte Carlo Localization, as the ROS 2 Navigation ecosystem has chosen to implement the standard particle filter localization algorithm, is not a surprising choice for this thesis, even with the competition from learned localization methods. Macenski et al.,\ writing from the maintainers' perspective on the ROS 2 Navigation stack, describe a comparison between various localization techniques currently in use in the ROS 2 space, including the preferred particle filter approach \cite{macenski2023fromdesks}. The summary provided serves as both background for the work in this thesis and a justification for it continuing to use the standard AMCL implementation in favor of more modern approaches. The key advantage of particle filter localization, as described by the authors, is its well-defined failure mode and the ability to produce meaningful covariance estimates for downstream safety-critical systems – a property that is explicitly used in the recovery state machine from Section~6.2 via the $\sigma_{\text{amcl}}$ parameter.

In turn, the common cause for localization failure in most AMCL-specific literature is the premature convergence of the particle filter to a single pose estimate after a kidnapping scenario, as the robot accumulates evidence in favor of a particular location before having the opportunity to explore the environment. Shi et al.\ suggest an approach to alleviate this problem by supplementing the standard particle filter with a separate visual localization estimate that can serve as a reference frame to reject implausible particles, particularly when visual features in the environment are sparse and visually similar, as one might expect in a warehouse setting \cite{shi2022vision}. The two-stage approach described in Section~6.2 is similar, but more limited – it does not attempt to observe the environment from new poses using additional sensors, only using the time between teleports to rotate in place and accumulate new laser scan data. First, the local localization recovery is attempted using this updated information (Stage~1) – if that fails within the twenty-two second budget, the process is repeated after a full global particle resampling step (Stage~2). In other words, the approach relies on the robot's ability to move on its own and observe new landmarks, while Shi et al.\ attempt to localize using extra visual information right away. The limitation of this approach is that the robot must move on its own, without any guidance, possibly exposing it to other failure modes before the particle filter resumes functioning. A slow but steady drift in the robot's position estimate, for example, would not be caught by the covariance check and the subsequent $\sigma_{\text{threshold}}$ check in Section~6.2 unless the robot physically moves to a new landmark that the particle filter can observe and update its estimate based on that. It is unclear whether such an issue would manifest in the constrained environment of a warehouse setting, which is why the eighteen-run evaluation campaign described in Chapter~8 did not attempt to differentiate between the two.

\section{Energy-Aware and Battery-Constrained Task (Re-)Allocation in Mobile Robot Fleets}

Battery-aware planning for mobile robot fleets is a sufficiently developed subfield to have its own warring camps. Most of the relevant literature is predicated on the realistic (if commercial) assumptions that a robot can and will return to a charging station at some point. Notomista et al.\ develop an online, continuously updated task allocation framework in which individual robot trajectories are optimized to the lowest energy expenditure possible for their set of constraints, explicitly prioritizing extended autonomy over single-batch completions \cite{notomista2022resilient}. Likewise, Djenadi and Mendil\ propose a similar energy-aware allocation with upfront mission planning that favors lower energy expenditures as a means to the same end of extended fleet autonomy \cite{djenadi2022energyaware}. Both of these approaches would have been incompatible with the battery model described in this thesis, if for no reason other than Section~6.1.1 explicitly details the choice to avoid return-to-charge behaviors in favor of testing the lifetime reallocation policy from Section~6.6 on guaranteed hardware failure. Online, continuous energy-aware allocation as described by the papers cited above would have in effect bypassed the need for the policies in Section~6.6 by eliminating the depletion scenario altogether before it could occur, with robots using their low-energy allocation to shed their heaviest tasks preemptively before reaching the failure threshold in Equation~\eqref{eq:absolute_failure_threshold}.

Meanwhile, the battery-proportional split described in this thesis is closer to a one-time, deterministic rebalancing than to continuous online allocation. It makes a single adjustment on the fly when a peer failure has been detected and computes a proportional share for each surviving agent based on their current SOC, rather than a continuously updated value function. It is a much less sophisticated tool than either of the methods described by Notomista et al.\ or the fleet-level planning and charging degradation analysis practiced in recent AMR literature \cite{djenadi2022energyaware}, but sufficient for the purpose described in Section~8.3 of answering the question of how quickly and fairly a fixed-size fleet could absorb the loss of one of its members without centralized coordination.

\section{Handcrafted Texture Descriptors versus Learned Representations for Edge-Deployed Visual Inspection}

The surface-defect detection literature has seen a clear shift away from handcrafted feature extraction in favor of either CNN-based or, more recently, transformer-based architectures over the last several years. The prevailing consensus in recent surveys is that standard feature extraction is best applied to either relatively simple binary image classification tasks or to serve as a lighter pre-processing stage for CNNs with significantly deeper architectures \cite{hussain2023custom}. The few works concerned specifically with warehouse infrastructure make this shift in both directions: at the algorithmic level, Hussain and Hill\ describe developing a custom CNN architecture for pallet racking damage detection, while also noting its lightweight design is a feature for deployment on embedded vision hardware \cite{hussain2023custom}. However, this thesis' choice to pursue the LBP/HOG pipeline described in Section~5.5 is justified less by considerations of accuracy and more by the shared CPU resource constraints with the AMCL, EKF, and Nav2 stack: the approximately $1.2\,\text{ms}$ budget derived in Section~5.5.4 would not realistically be available to a convolutional pipeline on a WSL2 host without specialized acceleration hardware. The choice to pursue a classical signal processing approach was dictated by the need for a frame budget on the same processing core that could accommodate even a lightweight CNN.

The challenge of obtaining an orthogonal view on multi-tier industrial racking systems is a natural extension of the limitations of planar differential drive robots to low clearance environments. One way to overcome the limitation is to transition to multi-dimensional or collaborative aerial inspection frameworks, a direction which several logistics warehousing works have pursued to enable full surface coverage and active sensor alignment. Tsiakas et al.\ describe one such effort to utilize indoor-certified UAVs for visual inspection of high-bay storage cells by leveraging inherent flight dynamics to provide the orthogonal view needed for comprehensive 3D coverage \cite{tsiakas2024autonomous}. However, the same literature notes the significant trade-offs in terms of aerodynamics-ground cross-coupling, payload limitations, and shorter battery cycles inherent to the approach. The time-based UAV proxy state machine developed in Section~4.3 and Section~5.3 of this thesis is, in a sense, an intermediate approach that seeks to replicate the benefits of the orthogonal view while being implemented on a single robot by utilizing time-encoding during the robot's stop-and-scan phase. The hybrid approach preserves the benefit of decoupling the camera's local reference frame from the robot's body frame to avoid perspective distortion of handcrafted features such as those in LBP and HOG descriptors \cite{tsiakas2024autonomous}, while avoiding the added mechanical and computational complexity of a ground-aerial robotic coalition.

This trade-off, however, is not strictly mechanical: one reason why feature crafting has not been entirely displaced by CNNs is that it is often competitive with deep learning methods on relevant metrics, particularly when computational constraints are taken into account. Sen et al.\ attempt to identify the minimal set of classical descriptors for image classification by supplementing permutation entropy with HOG and LBP features in a feature vector subsequently classified by a linear SVM. They find that the resulting 780-dimensional descriptor compares favorably to standard deep learning methods on standard benchmark data sets, both in terms of accuracy and training time, while also retaining theoretical interpretability advantages \cite{sen2025feature}. This aligns closely with what this thesis aims to achieve in Section~6.7.3 by specifying a perceptual module whose LBP block weights are interpretable enough to allow for the XAI-type backprojection in Equation~\eqref{eq:xai_back_projection}. The equivalence would hold true for a linear SVM on handcrafted features and a CNN feature extractor for most subsequent analysis steps. Similarly, a survey on embedded vision and TinyML vision anomalies detection identifies lightweight classical classifiers such as linear SVMs and isolation forests as frequently preferred options in resource-limited settings, where they offer the best combination of latency, memory footprint, and battery efficiency for on-device anomaly detection \cite{benmachiche2026realtime}. This thesis' preference for classical signal processing and handcrafted features over CNNs is therefore not an outlier, but rather one of several options for addressing domain-specific deployment constraints.

\section{Explainable AI and Analytical Redundancy for Safety-Critical Edge Classifiers}

Explainability in robotics typically takes the form of model-agnostic, post-hoc explanations, where a classifier is treated as a black box and queried with carefully crafted perturbations to approximate the contribution of individual features to a prediction. Sakai and Nagai,\ in their survey of explainable autonomous robots, observe that this model-agnostic generality comes at a cost: ``a computational overhead'' which renders ``few of the surveyed systems provide an explanation generation mechanism operating on the robot,'' to quote one relevant finding \cite{sakai2022explainable}. The XAI mechanism of Section~6.7.3 avoids the overhead by design rather than optimization: since the primary classifier is a linear SVM with a known feature space, attribution weights can be back-projected on the $128\times128$ image plane with a single matrix multiplication, at the approximately $1.2\,\text{ms}$ runtime of that very section, rather than through successive model queries. The price of this approach is visible in Table~6.3: while the closed-form solution to the back-projection task is always valid, the resulting attribution heatmap is only useful while the linear SVM is in active use, whereas the non-linear XGBoost classifier has no equivalent back-projection shortcut.

This fallback mechanism, in turn, exemplifies a design pattern in its own right: Myllyaho et al.\ interview software architects about design patterns in machine learning fault tolerance and find that ``implementing dual models or [redundant] ensembles'' where ``inference is rerouted to a structurally different subordinate model'' once the primary one is known to have failed is a common practice, if one of the rare ones, with a detailed quote available in \cite{myllyaho2022misbehaviour}. And one reason it is a rare practice stems from the limitations of the same design surface: in Section~8.5, 7278 scans across eighteen runs were processed by the primary SVM with no single one triggering the XGBoost fallback, which renders the behavior of the latter undocumented rather than a well-tested production capability, a subtlety which was noted and elaborated upon in that section.

\section{Simulation Substrates and Deterministic Orchestration for Multi-Robot ROS 2 Systems}

The decision process leading to the gate-based launch sequencing of Section~7.2 is a consequence of the recent turn of the ROS 2 community towards emphasizing reproducible experiments. Macenski et al.,\ in the maintainer-authored survey that has been mentioned in Section~3.4, describe the changes to Nav2 as a response to ``reliability requirements'' that ``ROS 1 tools were not developed for,'' and specifically name ``timing and startup determinism'' as areas of interest \cite{macenski2023fromdesks}. At the time of writing, a more specific, narrower response to the same stimulus is represented by Arena~4.0, a benchmarking environment proposed by Shcherbyna et al.,\ which formalizes the generation of environments and evaluation metrics specific to ROS 2 navigation research in an effort to facilitate comparisons between papers \cite{shcherbyna2024arena}. However, none of these works address the specific failure mode that leads to Section~7.2.1: a launch file which sequences its nodes through hardcoded \texttt{TimerAction} objects has no way to distinguish between a node that has received the topics it subscribed to and is able to proceed and a node which never receives the topics but nonetheless completes its launch timer, for a launch tree similar to the one described in that section has no notion of preconditions beyond ``has this node existed for X ms?''

Meanwhile, the practical considerations of executing fast, tight navigation maneuvers within the confines of a warehouse aisle have specific implications for the selection of a local planner. The shift within the ROS 2 Navigation working group from classical path-following to velocity-regulated approaches, represented by the Regulated Pure Pursuit (RPP) controller described by Macenski et al.\ \cite{macenski2023regulated}, reflects the growing awareness of the limitations of classical path-following approaches in confined spaces: by shaping the lookahead distance as a function of the instantaneous forward speed and reducing the linear velocity to match the angular requirements of a curving path, RPP reduces the amplitude of the steering oscillations and control-loop instability that appear characteristical to conventional Pure Pursuit controllers. In the context of a non-holonomic differential chassis driving within the tight confines of a warehouse, with a 1 cm safety margin around the local costmap inflation layer as described in Section~5.4, the curvature-based deceleration component of RPP becomes a necessary stability feature which prevents excessive wheelslip on the smooth concrete floor of a typical warehouse.

The same context drives the decision to implement the gate-based readiness polling mechanism of Section~7.2.2. It is a more narrow alternative to implementing a general-purpose, multi-robot benchmarking environment of the sort proposed by Shcherbyna et al.\ \cite{shcherbyna2024arena}: none of the surveyed toolchains provide a way to express, as a generic launch primitive, the readiness-dependent launch gate logic which waits for a named Gazebo world to load, a \texttt{/clock} publisher to appear on the master, a set of topic publishers to match the expected mask for each of the three robots, and a set of Nav2 lifecycle nodes to reach the active state, whereas implementing four small polling scripts and connecting them through the \texttt{OnProcessExit} interface (described in detail in Section~7.2.3) was easier than adapting an existing, general-purpose launch framework to the specific needs of a three-robot simulation within a single WSL2 instance. This is another instance of the general trend apparent throughout the works surveyed above: the multi-robot launch sequencing area of the ROS 2 toolchain is not yet mature enough to provide robust, generic primitives, and most solutions targeting it are specific to a particular use case (such as the three-robot warehouse navigation scenario of this thesis) and operate within a limited threat model.

\section{Synthesis: Positioning of the Present Work and Identified Gaps}

Read across the seven threads of analysis reviewed in this chapter, a common theme emerges: each subfield has arrived, independently of the others, to a general-purpose alternative to the solutions selected in this thesis. More specifically, auction-based resource allocation supersedes the heuristic allocation sweep, graph-based or token-based mutual exclusion replaces the TTL-based lease overwrite mechanism, continuous behavioral fault prediction displaces the two-channel heartbeat mechanism, sensor fusion replaces the covariance-thresholded spin-to-rescue, continuous power-optimized allocation overrides the one-time proportional allocation, and perception pipelines based on deep learning replace the handcrafted visual descriptors. In each case, a more general-purpose approach was available and declined in favor of alternatives that are narrower in scope and, in many cases, have been superseded by later advances. But the reason for this trend is apparent from the first paragraph of this chapter: the target deployment scenario is a small, homogeneous three-robot fleet operating on a constrained platform with no capacity to utilize the extra computational resources needed to run any of the more sophisticated alternatives.

At the same time, this cost is not without its costs, and they appear in the experimental section of Chapter~8 in the details which illustrate where this particular solution stack is at its weakest. First of all, this applies to the analytical redundancy mechanism described in Section~8.5 and the passive heartbeat-timeout channel of Section~8.3: the results of Section~8.5 show that the channel and the analytical redundancy were never actually required across eighteen runs, which means that the behaviors of these components under actual fault conditions remain untested and can only be assumed to work based on previous experience with similar mechanisms. Secondly, the same remark can be applied, although to a lesser extent, to the deadlock-avoidance mechanism of Section~8.5: the liveness guarantees derived from the reservation graph approach described in Section~3.2 apply to the lease-overwrite mechanism of Section~5.2 no more than the absence of deadlock does. Taken together, the picture that emerges from this review is not one of a novel algorithmic contribution competing head-to-head with the state of the art on any single axis. It is instead an integration exercise: a deliberately conservative selection from across seven active research threads, each choice biased toward determinism, low computational overhead, and empirical validation over formal guarantees, assembled into a single decentralized fleet and stress-tested as a whole under the fault-injection campaign of Chapter~8. Whether that particular combination of trade-offs generalizes past a three-robot, single-host WSL2 deployment is a question this thesis does not attempt to answer, and it is returned to explicitly in the future-work discussion of Chapter~9.
